Week 4, Evals. How often can a consultant use the answer?

Summary

My tool proposes a maturity level for one cybersecurity control from a client's documents, with exact quotes and the documents it used. The consultant makes the final decision. This week I built an eval harness for the Week 3 endpoint, which assesses one control for one fictional client.

I read fifty answers by hand and would not accept 24 of them. The harness scores the endpoint on 52 cases, compares every run with a saved baseline, and runs with one command. The headline is documents_found: 0.85, 95% CI [0.77, 0.92] on 47 cases. All data is synthetic.

The video is the demo for the whole tool so far: the problem, the user, how the tool works with the decision behind each step, how good it is today, one failure, what each week taught me, what comes next. This page follows the list of deliverables.

Taxonomy and hand scores

I read fifty traces from the Week 3 endpoint: the 10 retrieval cases, the 6 goldens and 34 rows from the Week 2 data, each turned into a fictional client of about 150 documents. I would not accept 24 of the 50 answers and would accept 26.

My rule: I accept an answer if a consultant could use it as a proposal, meaning the main conclusion is reasonable and it rests on the evidence of this case.

Calibration and whether I used the judge

The judge, judge_level_basis on Opus 5.5, checks failure #2: is each level backed by the key condition that separates it from the level below.

  • Separation on the four settled goldens: oracle (the golden answers) 1.00, echo 0.00, a wrong answer 0.00.
  • Agreement with my 50 verdicts: 0.62, chance floor 0.51. The first rubric scored 0.50 against a floor of 0.50.

Agreement is weak (the course's own example calls 0.60 not used), so the judge sets no number I report. It stays in run.py as an extra column, to see whether it improves once the decision rules are written. It catches only 2 of the 8 level failures in failures.md #2.

The decision row

From decisions.md, Week 4, second row.

Decision: Headline: documents_found, 0.85, 95% CI [0.77, 0.92] on 47 cases (52 cases in all, with goldens 7 and 8), saved in use_case/evals/baseline.json (compare() PASS). Two of the 52 answers crashed the app because the model returned invalid JSON; they count as 0, as the course harness does. On the 50 cases of 1 and 2 Nov, before goldens 7 and 8, the same scorer gave 0.90 both times. The judge (judge_level_basis, Opus 5.5) sets no number I report: its agreement is weak (the course's own example calls 0.60 not used). It stays in run.py as an extra column, to see whether it improves once the decision rules are written.

What you rejected: The judge as the headline: the first rubric failed agreement (0.50 against a chance floor of 0.50); the second passed separation (settled goldens 1.00, echo and a wrong answer 0.00) but agrees with my 50 verdicts only 0.62 against a floor of 0.51, and catches only 2 of the 8 level failures in failures.md #2. Step 5 scenarios, because the app answers one request and holds no conversation.

Why: It traces to the top line of failures.md: the search misses an important document. That also answers the Week 3 condition on missed documents: they are the top failure, so the next change is the search and the classifier, measured with run.py. It does not cover whether the level is right (only six of the eight goldens have a settled level), the reasoning (the judge column, which misses a level 1 given for lack of evidence and levels set too low), or real client documents (synthetic, short, three controls). Answers vary between runs, and on 47 cases the interval is about eight points each way, so Week 8 cannot show a smaller gain.

What would change your mind: Decision rules written per level (the key condition, level 1, levels too low) and confirmed by the consultant, then a judge that passes agreement on them; structured output, so a bad reply cannot crash the app; or more cases, so the interval narrows.

Step 7, demo review with a real user

Done in writing on 2 November with the GRC consultant: the five-minute demo (problem, numbers, one failure, next steps) and three questions. There was no time for a live session before the deadline, so I sent her the demo and eight cases and she answered in writing. A two-hour session with her is booked for Thursday, 5 November.

  • What would you do differently on Monday? She would run a limited pilot with mandatory consultant review and measure the time really saved, including review and corrections.
  • What would convince you the number is wrong? An independent review of all 50 answers against rules agreed in advance that gives different results, with the exact version of every answer kept.
  • What did you expect to see? The assessment date, the original document one click away, and a view of which condition of the level is confirmed and which is not.

From eight examples she would not yet confirm that most failures are in the search; for that she wants the findings for all 50 grouped. She confirmed two rules: a missing record alone does not give level 1, and a level with an open check is fine only if the check does not decide the level. On the five cases where the 2 Nov answer had the same levels as the one I read on 1 Nov, her verdict matched mine four times.

What it changed: a decline that does not say what to ask the client for is now listed in failures.md as an issue to fix. Next I send her failures.md with all 50 grouped, write the decision rules with her, and plan how the pilot measures time.

Bring it to work

Nobody measures how often a control level in a self-assessment is right: a consultant sets it by hand, and nobody knows how often two consultants would set different levels for the same client. In golden 4 the reference assessment says 3 and the consultant says 2.

  • Data: fifty controls from finished self-assessments, where the consultant who worked with the client already set the final level. Those levels are the verdicts that already exist.
  • Model or endpoint: the same internal AI gateway, Opus as the judge and Sonnet as the app, so client documents and answers never leave our environment. One run.py makes 62 judge calls (12 to calibrate, 50 to score; the two crashed answers are not scored) and 52 app calls, 52 minutes [measured, 2 Nov], so it runs nightly, not on every commit.
  • Approval: client consent to use their finished self-assessments for measurement, access to the archive of those assessments, then a deployment review with DevOps, and security and privacy reviews.
